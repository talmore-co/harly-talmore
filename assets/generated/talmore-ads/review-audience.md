# Synthetic audience review of Talmore Robot Operator ads

16 September 2026

## Status and method

This is an AI simulation of six distinct candidate perspectives, not a focus group with real participants. Every reaction, preference and application decision below is hypothetical. The personas use invented circumstances, no actual personal data. Their preferences follow their stated experience and constraints, not demographic assumptions.

I read `study-brief.md`, both contact sheets and the asset README independently, without consulting other reviewers' notes. All 17 current variants were reviewed on the contact sheets. I also opened full PNG exports for A2, B2, B3, C2, C3, D2, E2 and F1 through F4 to inspect shortlisted concepts and concerns. The three-second discussion is a conceptual estimate of what a quick mobile-feed glance might communicate. No timed exposure, eye tracking, phone usability session or recall test occurred. Full-image inspection cannot establish mobile legibility.

For each persona, the sequence below separates initial ad interpretation, fuller reading and the effect of learning the brief's facts. In particular, minimum wage is known from the brief, not disclosed by any of these ads. The live posting's salary and training omissions are also brief-supplied facts, not independently checked here.

## Main finding

The strongest set is a mix of approaches. F1 identifies competitive experience well, F2 names relevant genres, and F3 gives non-esports hobbyists a route in. But B2 explains the work attitude more credibly, C2 makes the vacancy unmistakable, and E2 connects training with a visible control device without requiring a competitive identity.

The common weakness is substantive. A person can understand that Talmore is hiring and still not know what the robots do, whether the headset represents everyday work, or whether minimum-wage night work in Alabang is practical. More attractive hobby imagery cannot answer those questions.

## Six simulated candidate perspectives

### P1: Competitive FPS fresh graduate near Alabang

Invented profile: recent bachelor's graduate, regularly plays ranked FPS with a team, mostly uses keyboard and mouse, lives within a manageable commute of Alabang. Wants a first full-time role and can consider rotating nights. No VR experience.

- **Conceptual first glance.** F1's competitive hook and F2's FPS label command attention. Initial role guess is a gaming-related technology job, possibly game testing. The VR imagery does not immediately explain a real robot operation.
- **After reading.** Recognizes the degree requirement, fresh-graduate welcome in the broader variants, onsite location and training provision. F1 validates competitive experience. Controller-focused ads leave an unnecessary doubt about keyboard-and-mouse players.
- **Trust and decision.** A named location and clear shift language help. A posed headset image establishes neither an actual workplace nor actual equipment. Would investigate because of proximity and training. Learning the wage is minimum wage makes this a comparison with other accessible first jobs, not an automatic rejection or acceptance.
- **Questions.** What does a normal shift involve? Do keyboard-and-mouse FPS skills count? What is the exact rate and pay basis? How long is training, and is it paid?

Top three, in order:
1. **F1.** Direct recognition of competitive experience, with robot operations in the headline.
2. **F2.** FPS is more specific than generic controller skill; the genre label offsets the unfamiliar headset.
3. **C2.** The large job title and fresh-graduate invitation make it easy to recognize a real vacancy.

Bottom one: **B3.** The largest promise, "New skills start here," feels like a course promotion. The small role label requires extra work to understand the offer.

Imagined reaction: "The FPS one gets my attention. But am I controlling something real, or testing a game?"

### P2: Former esports participant seeking a career start

Invented profile: completed two years of college, previously played organized competitive FPS, now plays less often. Wants a dependable work routine and transferable experience. Open to nights after understanding the schedule.

- **Conceptual first glance.** F1 is the clearest invitation. Other gamer ads might seem aimed at currently active players. B2 reads as employment preparation rather than entertainment.
- **After reading.** "Current or former competitive player?" is unusually useful. The two-year college alternative resolves a concern that a degree is mandatory, but it is in a small gray line. Training lowers the barrier to changing fields.
- **Trust and decision.** B2's procedures, care and teamwork provide a credible bridge from competition to work. Would ask for a task description before investing much time. Minimum wage could be acceptable as a starting point only if the commute and hours work; the futuristic presentation must not imply premium technical pay or a promised career ladder.
- **Questions.** Is recent ranking required? How is former competitive experience assessed? What are the operating responsibilities and shift rotations? Is there an employment contract during training?

Top three, in order:
1. **F1.** Explicitly includes former players rather than leaving them to infer eligibility.
2. **B2.** Explains that work requires procedures and teamwork, which makes the transition more believable.
3. **F2.** Recognizes familiar genres without demanding professional status.

Bottom one: **C3.** "Curious about robotics?" sounds closer to general education than a concrete next job, and may raise engineering expectations.

Imagined reaction: "Former player is the part that matters to me. I still need to see what I would actually do all night."

### P3: Racing and flight-sim hobbyist with two years of college

Invented profile: completed two years of college, regularly uses a racing wheel and flight joystick, does not identify as competitive or esports. Interested in careful control, monitoring and learning procedures. Has a workable route to Alabang but needs advance shift information.

- **Conceptual first glance.** F2's genre names feel personally relevant. F1 may look like an invitation for a different group. The pictured gamepad and VR headset do not resemble this persona's usual controls.
- **After reading.** Finds the college alternative and recognizes potential fit. B2's care and procedures match the skills this persona values. None of the ads explicitly recognizes wheel or joystick experience, so the connection remains partly inferred.
- **Trust and decision.** Specific genres are more credible than a general claim that all gaming transfers to work. Would consider applying if the real tasks involve the advertised control skills. Wage disclosure is needed to judge the shift commute; owning hobby equipment says nothing about willingness to accept minimum wage.
- **Questions.** Do wheel and joystick experience count? Is VR experience necessary? How much of the shift is control work versus logs and monitoring? What are the actual start and finish times?

Top three, in order:
1. **F2.** Only headline explicitly naming both racing and flight sims.
2. **B2.** Procedures and care make a stronger occupational connection than speed alone.
3. **F3.** Hands-on controls feel relevant even though the supporting examples omit wheels and joysticks.

Bottom one: **F1.** "Competitive gamer?" invites avoidable self-exclusion for a regular sim user with relevant experience.

Imagined reaction: "I use a flight stick, not that controller. If that still counts, this could be worth reading."

### P4: RC, drone and Arduino hobbyist outside esports

Invented profile: bachelor's graduate, builds small Arduino projects and operates hobby RC vehicles and drones. Rarely plays video games. Wants practical technology work, but does not want to assume an operating role includes designing or repairing robots.

- **Conceptual first glance.** F3's hands-on headline attracts attention, although its headset and gamepad first suggest gaming. The RC and drone words carry the real invitation and need to survive mobile reduction.
- **After reading.** F3 recognizes part of this background. B2 makes the procedural nature more obvious. Arduino and robot-kit experience are absent from the ads despite being recognized in the questionnaire, so a maker who misses the RC line may assume the job is not for them.
- **Trust and decision.** More interested once the work is described as operating, documenting and escalating issues. C3's robotics wording attracts curiosity but risks suggesting robot building. Would not accept minimum-wage routine operation on the mistaken promise of engineering work. Might apply if the actual duties and schedule are suitable.
- **Questions.** Is this teleoperation, assembly, programming or equipment maintenance? Does hands-on experience count without gaming? What equipment will be used? Is basic English sufficient for the logs and escalation process?

Top three, in order:
1. **F3.** RC and drones provide the clearest evidence that experience outside gaming matters.
2. **B2.** Sets a more realistic expectation of procedural work and training.
3. **C2.** Plain role-first presentation is useful once the work has been explained.

Bottom one: **F1.** Esports-style identity is a poor invitation for this otherwise relevant candidate.

Imagined reaction: "RC and drones caught my eye. I would want to know whether 'robotics' means operating equipment or building it."

### P5: Female regular VR and gaming user

Invented profile: woman with a bachelor's degree, regularly uses VR motion controllers and plays games socially, does not compete. Can commute to Alabang and consider nights if the rotation is clear. Her equipment familiarity, not gender, drives the strongest creative preferences.

- **Conceptual first glance.** F4's VR question and E2's headset make the relevant experience obvious. E2 more directly says what she would learn. D2 feels approachable, but its ordinary gamepad is less specific to her strongest experience.
- **After reading.** Training and education requirements are understandable. F1 creates doubt about whether regular gaming is enough. Female imagery provides representation, but does not establish the team culture or working conditions.
- **Trust and decision.** Would investigate E2 or F4. The everyday styling is plausible, yet these are fictional portraits rather than employee evidence. Learning the minimum wage changes the decision to one about take-home pay and commute. Headset duration and breaks matter because recreational VR sessions do not establish comfort over a work shift.
- **Questions.** Is VR used continuously? What are the actual tasks and break arrangements? Are regular players welcome? Is equipment supplied, and what training is provided? What are the shift times and exact site location?

Top three, in order:
1. **E2.** Clear "Learn to operate robots" message plus recognizable motion controllers and fresh-graduate welcome.
2. **F4.** Most direct recognition of VR familiarity, with training prominent in the body.
3. **D2.** Human, face-visible alternative that avoids suggesting headset use throughout the job.

Bottom one: **F1.** Competitive wording makes a regular VR user question her fit despite relevant experience.

Imagined reaction: "I know those controls. I don't call myself competitive, though. Am I still who they're looking for?"

### P6: Practical applicant with commute and night-shift limits

Invented profile: completed two years of college, casual gamer without hands-on controller hobbies. Has a difficult multi-leg commute to Alabang and a fixed evening commitment. Wants predictable employment and cannot reliably rotate through nights. Low fit here comes chiefly from logistics, not an inferred lack of ability.

- **Conceptual first glance.** C2 looks most clearly like a vacancy. A2's bright block and controller may catch attention but do little to answer whether the job is practical. VR imagery could initially suggest home-based technology work if the footer is missed.
- **After reading.** The education requirement is met. Training makes the unfamiliar work less daunting. The onsite and rotating-night requirements then become decisive. Preferred gaming backgrounds do not mean this persona is prohibited from applying.
- **Trust and decision.** Values disclosure more than novelty. Would decline under the stated schedule constraints. Learning that pay is minimum wage reinforces the need to avoid an application based on unrealistic commute economics. No creative should try to overcome an actual availability mismatch.
- **Questions.** Where exactly is the facility? What are the shift start and finish times? What is the exact rate and pay basis? Is any fixed schedule available? The brief provides no basis to promise one.

Top three, in order, for useful communication rather than intent to apply:
1. **C2.** Large job title and repeated Alabang mention make the offer easiest to identify.
2. **B2.** Training and responsible work expectations help assess an unfamiliar role.
3. **E2.** The action-led headline is more understandable than a gamer-identity invitation.

Bottom one: **A2.** Visually strong, but abstract gaming language consumes space needed for practical decisions.

Imagined reaction: "I might qualify on education. The rotating nights are the problem, and I need the pay before planning that commute."

## Agreement and disagreement within the simulation

These are recurring modeled concerns, not respondent counts or population estimates.

- Hobby recognition opens the door, but the role needs a plain explanation. None of the personas should have to infer the difference between remote-controlled robots and remote employment.
- Training matters to beginners and experienced hobbyists alike. Knowing a game or controller does not establish knowledge of workplace procedures. The brief says the destination omits training, so the ad's reassurance may disappear at the point where a candidate tries to verify it.
- Salary is not a cosmetic omission. No exact rate appears, and no ad even states minimum wage. A reader may infer technical pay from robotics language. The personas differ on whether minimum wage might work for them, but all lack the information needed to decide from the ad.
- The footer is consistent and contains useful facts. It is also visually subordinate to the headline and image. At contact-sheet scale, the education line is especially easy to skip. Presence is not the same as quick recognition.
- F1 creates the sharpest disagreement. It is first choice for competitive and former-competitive personas, and bottom choice for several relevant noncompetitive personas. That makes it a specific invitation, not a universal replacement.
- F4 gives the VR regular immediate recognition but may suggest a prior-VR requirement to everyone else. E2's learning invitation is broader.
- Face-visible D2 offers a human connection; headset-on E2 offers a more recognizable tool cue. This exercise does not establish which attracts more attention in a live feed.
- C2 is useful to both a motivated fresh graduate and a person who should decline. Helping an unsuitable applicant recognize a schedule mismatch is a good outcome.

## Shortlist and concrete changes

### Shortlist

Core five concepts to refine: **B2, C2, E2, F1 and F3**. They cover work/training, role clarity, accessible VR learning, competitive experience and hands-on experience outside esports. This is a concept shortlist, not a recommendation to fund five simultaneous ad sets.

Keep **F2** as the next genre-specific challenger and **D2** as a face-visible comparison. F4 is relevant but overlaps E2 while imposing a narrower identity. The earlier B2 and C2 deserve places even though they lack the newer portrait treatment.

### Shared changes, in priority order

1. **Explain the job near the title.** Use confirmed wording such as "Operate remote-controlled robots from our Alabang facility." It connects the title to an action and resolves work-location ambiguity. In supporting copy, add one grounded duty such as following procedures and documenting activity. Do not invent a robot type or application.
2. **Make practical fit readable before the click.** Move "Onsite in Alabang, Muntinlupa" and "Rotating shifts, including nights" closer to the main message, using space currently spent on generic benefits or decorative arrows. Retain full-time status. Do not shrink them to make room for more hobby examples.
3. **Disclose the known pay level and obtain the missing detail.** "Minimum-wage role" is supported by the brief. Confirm the exact applicable rate and pay basis for candidate-facing copy and the destination. Do not estimate monthly pay or invent allowances, premiums or transport benefits. The omission is especially consequential beside aspirational robotics imagery.
4. **Bring education out of the gray fine print.** For example, "At least 2 years of college; fresh graduates welcome." Keep the precise qualification meaning. A reader who has two years of college should not stop after seeing bachelor's degree first.
5. **Carry training through to the destination.** Preserve "Training provided" and recommend adding that confirmed fact to the posting. Confirm duration and whether training is paid before stating either. No live edit was made for this review.
6. **Treat hobbies as routes in, not gates.** Supporting text can say "Gaming or hands-on controller experience welcome." For F1, retain the current/former line but explain on the destination that competition is an advantage, not an application requirement. Do not turn questionnaire preferences into eligibility exclusions.
7. **Use generated portraits as visual concepts, not workplace evidence.** Keep ordinary styling and male/female coverage. Do not call the subjects employees or suggest the pictured room and headset are Talmore's actual setup. No visible concept label is proposed. Explain confirmed working conditions in copy instead of asking the image to prove them.

### Variant-specific edits

- B2: replace "for operational tasks" with a plain robot-control explanation. Trade one generic list item for "Document activity and escalate issues." This better reflects the published responsibilities.
- C2: keep the large title. Replace "Your next application?" with a useful location line and shorten the paragraph to make room for what the operator does.
- E2: retain the learning headline and headset cue, but make the robot-control explanation more prominent than "Bring your focus." Avoid implying continuous headset wear.
- F1: preserve the competitive/current-or-former specificity. Put practical fit in stronger type, and avoid any suggestion that rank guarantees ability or hiring.
- F2: remove the decorative circular arrow and use that space for task clarity or training. Keep the genre names because they supply the distinctive value.
- F3: elevate "RC, drones and controller experience" into larger copy. Recognize robot kits or Arduino in supporting ad text or the destination without trying to cram every questionnaire option into the image. A confirmed appropriate visual could later improve the match; do not invent employer hardware.
- D2: retain the face-visible option for comparison, but add a concrete duty so the leisure pose does not carry the entire job story.

## Limits and next validation

Six invented personas cannot establish audience preferences, attention rates, trust levels or application behavior. These are structured hypotheses from one AI reviewer, not six independent human opinions. Persona ordering is constructed, not observed. The contact sheet may favor large headlines and encourage comparison that does not occur in an ordinary feed. Generated photos may appear more or less convincing to real candidates than they do here.

Unknown actual tasks, equipment, exact wage basis, training terms and commute routes limit the conclusions. No assessment here predicts CTR, CPA, hiring volume or employee retention. No gender-based effectiveness claim is supported, and appearance is not an eligibility criterion.

For real follow-up, show individual creatives at an actual mobile-feed size and ask candidates what job, location, schedule and qualifications they understood before explaining the role. Then disclose actual pay and training terms and ask what changes their willingness to apply. A genuine comprehension check should distinguish attraction from correct understanding and practical fit. Campaign assessment should follow suitable completed applications and downstream quality; a click alone does not establish success. The brief says the qualified-application threshold is disabled, so this review assumes no active QualifiedApplication optimization event.
